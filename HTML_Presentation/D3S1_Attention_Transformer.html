Where we are: three limits left over from Day 2

Words are unrelated symbols

To TF-IDF, "great" and "excellent" are as different as "great" and "banana". What the model learns about one word teaches it nothing about the other.

Context is short

An n-gram sees 1 or 2 words back. In "the movie that my friends said was boring turned out great", the word deciding the label is far from "movie".

One model per task

Every task needed its own labelled data and its own model, trained from zero. Nothing was reused.

Today fixes the first two. Sessions 2 and 3 fix the third.

From one-hot to dense vectors

One-hot (Day 1)

A vector as long as the vocabulary, all zeros except one 1.

great    = [0 0 1 0 0 ... 0]
excellent = [0 0 0 0 1 ... 0]

Dot product of any two different words: 0. No notion of similar.

Dense vector (embedding)

A short list of real numbers, typically 100 to 1024, learned from text.

great = [...]

Similar words get similar vectors, so what is learned for one word helps its neighbours.

A one-hot vector is a roll number: unique, but tells you nothing about the student. An embedding is a profile: marks in 100 subjects. Two students with similar profiles are similar, even if you never compared them directly.

The numbers on the right are the first 12 of the 100 numbers our word2vec model learned for "great" (next slide). No single number has a meaning on its own; the meaning is in how vectors relate to each other.

Where the numbers come from: you shall know a word by its company

word2vec (Mikolov et al., 2013) learns vectors by a simple task: predict a word from its neighbours. Words that appear in similar contexts end up with similar vectors.

"The acting was ___ and the plot was weak." Words that fit the blank: bad, poor, wooden, terrible. The model is pushed to give them similar vectors because they predict the same neighbours.

from gensim.models import Word2Vec
model = Word2Vec(sentences,           # list of token lists: all 50,000 IMDB reviews
                 vector_size=100,     # 100 numbers per word
                 window=5,           # neighbours = 5 words on each side
                 min_count=5)        # ignore words seen fewer than 5 times
model.wv.most_similar("great")

We trained it on the 50,000 IMDB reviews: . Real results on the next slide.

This is the CBOW variant (continuous bag of words): average the neighbours' vectors and predict the middle word; the skip-gram variant predicts the neighbours from the middle word. Training is a small neural network with one hidden layer; the hidden layer weights are the vectors. The famous quote is from the linguist J. R. Firth (1957).

Explore real word vectors trained on IMDB

Click through great, terrible, boring, actor. Then show the pairs: movie/film 0.94 (almost synonyms in reviews), and good/bad 0.77, higher than great/excellent. That is the main weakness: antonyms appear in the same contexts ("the acting was good/bad"), so context-based vectors put them close. For sentiment that is a real problem, and one reason fine-tuning (Session 3) matters. Also funny and scary are each other's nearest neighbour: both describe genre films in the same position of a sentence.

Arithmetic on meaning, and where it breaks

Directions carry meaning

actor − man + woman →

king − man + woman →

Real results from our IMDB vectors. The difference "woman minus man" is roughly a direction in the space.

One vector per word

"The plot was light" and "the light was too dark in every scene" get the same vector for light. A static vector mixes all senses of a word.

good − better + bad → : not "worse". Analogies work for some relations only.

What we need next: a vector for a word in this sentence, built from the words around it. That is exactly what attention computes.

The king/queen analogy works only weakly on movie reviews (queen at 0.59, barely above jane and carmen) because royalty is rare in reviews; on Wikipedia-trained vectors it is much clearer. The polysemy problem (one vector for all senses) is what contextual embeddings such as BERT solve: the vector for light depends on the sentence.

Attention in one sentence

To build the vector for a word, look at every word in the sentence, decide how relevant each one is, and take a weighted average of their information.

"The movie my friends said was boring turned out great." To understand great, the most useful word is movie (what is great?), not "friends" or "boring". Attention can give "movie" a high weight even though it is 7 words away.

A student in a group project with a question (the query). Every teammate has a badge saying what they know (the key) and their actual notes (the value). The student compares the question with every badge, then reads mostly the notes of the best matches.

Query, key and value are the three roles every word plays. A word asks a question (query), advertises what it can answer (key), and offers its content (value). All three are made from the word's own vector by multiplying with three learned matrices.

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q KT / √dk ) V

Dot product

Large when two vectors point the same way: query and key match.

√d scaling

With d = 64 numbers per vector, dot products get large; softmax would put almost all weight on one word. Dividing keeps it smooth.

Softmax

escore / sum of escore. Every weight is positive and they add to 1.

The formula is from "Attention Is All You Need" (Vaswani et al., 2017). Q, K and V are matrices with one row per word, so one matrix product computes all the scores of all words at once. That is why attention is fast on GPUs.

Compute attention yourself

Made-up numbers, small enough to check on paper (d = 4). Pick the word that asks the question.

Pick "good": its query matches the key of "not" and of "movie" best, so its new vector is mostly made of their values. That is how the model can learn that "not" changes "good". Then switch off the scaling and push the sharpness slider: softmax becomes winner-takes-all, which makes training unstable. In a real model the numbers are learned, not chosen; we chose them to make the pattern visible.

Self-attention: every word does this at once

Self

Queries, keys and values all come from the same sentence. Each of the n words attends to all n words, including itself.

Result: n new vectors, each a mix of the whole sentence. The vector for "good" now knows about "not".

Where Q, K, V come from

Three learned weight matrices:

Q = X WQ
K = X WK
V = X WV

X: one row per word (its embedding). Training sets WQ, WK, WV.

Cost: n words means n × n scores. A 512-token text gives 262,144 scores per head per layer. This is why models have a maximum input length (BERT: 512 tokens).

The n-squared cost is the main practical limit of transformers and the reason long documents are truncated or split (Session 3 for IMDB reviews, Session 4 for news articles). Many research models reduce it, but the standard models we use have the limit.

Multi-head attention: several questions at once

One attention step answers one kind of question. A word needs several: what is my subject, what modifies me, what does "it" refer to?

word vectors Xn × 768
→
12 headseach with its own WQ, WK, WV, size 64
→
concatenate12 × 64 = 768
→
output WOmixes the heads, n × 768

Each head can learn a different pattern. Studies of trained BERT (Clark et al., 2019) found heads that follow syntax: one attends from objects to their verbs, another from determiners to their nouns, the dependency arcs of Day 2.

Numbers from BERT-base: 768-number vectors, 12 heads of 64. The total work is about the same as one big head; the heads just split it.

"What does BERT look at?" by Clark, Khandelwal, Levy and Manning (2019) is a readable paper for students who want evidence that heads learn linguistic structure. Not every head is interpretable; many attend to punctuation or the separator token.

Word order: positional encodings

Attention is a weighted sum, and a sum ignores order: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" would give the same result. So a position vector is added to each word vector before the first layer.

The original transformer used fixed sine and cosine waves of different wavelengths (shown here): low dimensions change fast with position, high dimensions slowly, like the hands of a clock. Nearby positions get similar vectors. BERT instead learns one vector per position (0 to 511) during pretraining; the idea is the same.

One transformer block

input vectorsword + position
→
multi-head self-attentionmix information across words
→
add & normalisekeep the input, rescale
→
feed-forwardtwo linear layers per word
→
add & normaliseoutput vectors

Attention

Words exchange information. The only place where words see each other.

Feed-forward

Each word's vector is processed on its own (768 → 3072 → 768 in BERT). Most of the parameters live here.

Add & norm

"Residual connection": add the input back to the output, so a block can only adjust the vector, not destroy it. Makes deep stacks trainable.

Stack the block: BERT-base uses 12 blocks, the original translation transformer 6 in the encoder and 6 in the decoder. The output has the same shape as the input, so blocks stack like bricks.

Input and output of a block are both n vectors of size 768, which is what makes stacking possible. After 12 blocks each word's vector has been mixed with the whole sentence 12 times: these are contextual embeddings.

Why transformers replaced recurrent networks

Recurrent network (RNN, LSTM)Transformer
How it readsone word at a time, carrying a memory vector forwardall words at once
Long-range linksinformation from word 1 must pass through every step to reach word 50; it fadesword 50 attends to word 1 directly, one step
Training speedsteps depend on each other: hard to paralleliseone big matrix product: fast on GPUs
Weaknessslow, forgetscost grows with n × n, needs a length limit; needs lots of data

Parallel training is what made it possible to train on billions of words, and training on billions of words is what made pretrained models possible (Session 2).

LSTMs (1997) improved on plain RNNs with gates that decide what to keep, and with attention added (2015) they were the best translation models until 2017. The transformer paper's title, "Attention Is All You Need", is the claim that the recurrence can be dropped entirely.

Decoder, two kinds of attention

Masked self-attention over the words written so far (it may not peek at future words), and cross-attention to the encoder's vectors: "which source words matter for the next target word?"

Same design, many tasks

Translation (English → Marathi), summarisation (article → summary, Session 4), question answering, paraphrasing. BART and T5 are encoder-decoders.

Three families: encoder only (BERT: understand text, Sessions 2-3), encoder-decoder (BART, T5: text to text, Session 4), decoder only (GPT, Gemini: continue text, Session 5).

The decoder generates the way Day 2's n-gram model generated text: predict a distribution over the next token, pick one, append, repeat. The difference is what the prediction is conditioned on: the whole source sentence through cross-attention plus everything generated so far, instead of two previous words.

English is subject-verb-object, Marathi and Hindi are subject-object-verb: "I am going to school" → "मी शाळेत जात आहे". Reordering is exactly where rule systems and phrase tables struggled and attention helps.

Google Translate used phrase-based SMT from 2006 and switched to neural translation in 2016. Translation quality is scored with BLEU, which counts matching n-grams between the system output and human reference translations; we meet it with ROUGE in Session 4. The original transformer reached 28.4 BLEU on WMT 2014 English-German, beating all earlier systems.

Common mistakes in this session

"Similar vectors means same meaning." good and bad are close (0.77) in our vectors. Similar context, not similar meaning.

Forgetting the softmax. Raw dot products are not weights; they can be negative and do not add to 1.

Thinking attention knows word order. Without positional encodings it does not.

"Attention weights explain the model." They show where information flowed in one layer, not why the final answer was chosen.

Mixing up the families. BERT does not generate text; GPT-style models are not bidirectional.

Ignoring the length limit. Text beyond 512 tokens is cut off silently by BERT tokenizers.

The attention-as-explanation point is a known debate in research ("Attention is not Explanation", Jain and Wallace, 2019). It is safe to say attention shows information flow, not reasons.

Recap, and what Session 2 starts with

  • Dense vectors put words with similar contexts close together; they still mix senses and confuse antonyms.
  • Attention: scores q·k, scale by √d, softmax, weighted sum of values. Every word can use every other word.
  • Transformer block: multi-head self-attention + feed-forward, each with add & norm; positions added at the start; blocks stack.
  • Encoder-decoder maps one sequence to another; NMT replaced rule-based and statistical translation.
Still unsolved: where do the input vectors come from, and what about words never seen in training (Day 1's OOV problem)? And who trains all those weight matrices? Session 2: subword tokens, and pretraining BERT on billions of words.

The handoff: a transformer is an empty architecture until it is trained. Session 2 is about the input side (tokens) and the training side (pretraining).

d = 64. A raw score is 24. What is the scaled score?

√64 = 8, so 24 / 8 = 3.

3. Count the scores

A 128-token input, 12 heads, 12 layers. How many attention scores?

128 × 128 × 12 × 12 = 2,359,296.

4. Think

Why are "good" and "bad" close in word2vec space?

They occur in the same contexts ("the acting was ___"), and word2vec learns only from context.
