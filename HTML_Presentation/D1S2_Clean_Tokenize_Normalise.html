First, two words we must define: token and vocabulary

Take a tiny text and count by hand.

thecatsatonthemattheend

Token = every occurrence

There are 8 tokens. "the" appears three times, and we count it three times.

Type (vocabulary entry) = every distinct word

There are 5 types: the, cat, sat, on, mat, end... wait, that is 6. Count again: 6 types.

A library. Tokens are all the books on the shelves, including copies. The vocabulary is the catalogue: one line per different title.

I made an error on purpose: counting by hand is easy to get wrong. This is why the notebook always counts with code, using len(set(tokens)) for types and len(tokens) for tokens.

Do the counting live. Most students say 5 types first. The deliberate "wait, that is 6" is a gentle way to show that code is more reliable than counting by eye. If you prefer, say it was not deliberate, it is still a useful moment.

Vocabulary is also called "types" in linguistics. We will use "vocabulary" and "distinct words" interchangeably.

Why vocabulary size matters: it becomes the width of the matrix

In Session 5, every distinct word becomes one column of numbers. Every review becomes one row.

moviemoviesMoviemovie!film
review 120010
review 201103

Four of these five columns are really the same idea: movie. The model must learn the same fact four times, from fewer examples each time. A wide, repetitive vocabulary means a slower, hungrier, noisier model.

The measurement we will use all session: how many distinct words are left after each step. Fewer, without losing meaning, is better.

This is the motivation slide for the entire session. Fix the idea: cleaning, tokenizing and normalising are all ways of merging columns that mean the same thing. The danger is merging columns that do not mean the same thing (e.g. removing "not").

Reading a regular expression, one character at a time

To remove punctuation we use re.sub(pattern, replacement, text): find everything matching the pattern, replace it with the replacement.

re.sub(r"[^a-z0-9\s]", "", text)
[ ... ]a set: match any one character that is inside the brackets
^ (first inside)not: flip the set, match any character that is not in it
a-zthe range of lowercase letters
0-9the range of digits
\sany whitespace: space, tab, newline
r"..."a raw string: Python leaves the backslash alone so \s reaches the regex engine

Read the whole pattern aloud: "any single character that is not a lowercase letter, a digit or whitespace", replace with nothing. So "great!!! :)" becomes "great ".

This slide is not a regex course. It is just enough to read the cleaning patterns in the notebook without fear. Session 3 comes back to regular expressions properly, with a tester and the link to finite automata.

A common confusion: ^ means "start of string" outside brackets and "not" at the start inside brackets. Mention this once and move on.

Stage 3: what is a word? Count the words in this sentence

I don't like New York's e-mail rules.

How many words? Some say 6, some 7, some 8, some 10. Who is right?

  • Is "don't" one word or two? (do + n't, and n't carries the negation)
  • Is "New York's" one, two or three? (New York + 's)
  • Is "e-mail" one word or two?
  • Is the final "." a word?
Honest answer: there is no universal rule. A tokenizer is a decision procedure, and "token" means "the unit this procedure outputs". We pick the procedure that suits the task.

Let the room argue for a minute. Linguists disagree too. The word "token" is deliberately different from "word": it is just whatever piece our tokenizer produces, whether or not it is a dictionary word. Punctuation marks and n't are tokens.

Three families of tokenizer, from simplest to smartest

1. Whitespace split

text.split()

Cut at spaces. Fast. Leaves punctuation stuck to words: "dry!!!", "all,".

2. Regex

re.findall(r"\w+", text)

Keep runs of letters, digits and underscore. Removes punctuation but also shreds can't into can + t and $5.99 into 5 + 99.

3. Rule-based

word_tokenize(text)

A long list of hand-written rules, tuned on real text. Handles contractions, abbreviations, numbers. NLTK and spaCy both work this way.

Each family fixes a failure of the one before it. We pick the cheapest one that does not break our task. Next slide: try all of them on the same sentences.

Present the three as a progression: each one exists because the previous one fails in a specific way. Mention that modern transformer models use a fourth family, subword tokenizers like BPE, which we meet on Day 3. They solve a different problem: unknown words.

Pick a sentence (1 to 8). Look for places where the rows disagree and decide which you would trust.

How a rule-based tokenizer decides: four rules in order

NLTK's word_tokenize uses rules like these (simplified). Apply them to Don't go; it's 2 hours of nothing.

RuleResult after the rule
0. StartDon't go; it's 2 hours of nothing.
1. Split off punctuation ; : , ? ! as separate tokensDon't go ; it's 2 hours of nothing.
2. Split a final period from the last wordDon't go ; it's 2 hours of nothing .
3. Split contractions: n't and 's become their own tokensDo n't go ; it 's 2 hours of nothing .
4. Split on spaces['Do', "n't", 'go', ';', 'it', "'s", '2', 'hours', 'of', 'nothing', '.']

That final list is exactly what the playground printed for sentence 5. Rule 3 is why NLTK writes Do + n't: the negation becomes a visible token that later steps can find. Rule 2 knows not to split the period in "Dr." because of a list of known abbreviations.

This is a simplification of the Penn Treebank tokenizer, which NLTK's word_tokenize follows. The real implementation is a sequence of regular expression substitutions, in this order, and then a split on spaces. Show the idea: tokenization is "insert spaces in the right places, then split".

Tokenizing sentences: why not just split on the period?

Sometimes we need sentences first (for sentence-level labels, or summarising). Try the naive way:

text = "Dr. Smith went to Washington. He arrived at 3 p.m. on Jan. 5th! Was it worth it? Mr. Lee said no."
text.split(". ")
['Dr', 'Smith went to Washington', 'He arrived at 3 p.m', 'on Jan', '5th! Was it worth it? Mr', 'Lee said no.']

Six pieces, only one correct, and "?" and "!" were ignored. A period does three jobs: ends a sentence, ends an abbreviation (Dr.), sits inside a number (3.5).

from nltk.tokenize import sent_tokenize
sent_tokenize(text)
['Dr. Smith went to Washington.', 'He arrived at 3 p.m. on Jan. 5th!', 'Was it worth it?', 'Mr. Lee said no.']

sent_tokenize uses Punkt, a model trained on text to learn which words are abbreviations. Four sentences, correct. Rules plus statistics beat a single rule.

The first output is the real result of that split call (shortened by one trailing period on the last piece). Punkt is unsupervised: it learned abbreviation lists from raw text by noticing words that are often followed by a period but not by a capitalised word.

For the review dataset we do not need sentence splitting much, but it will come back in Day 3 for summarisation.

Stage 4: one idea, many word forms

After tokenizing, these are four different tokens (four columns):

runrunsrunningran

To a person they are one idea. We want to map all of them to one form. Two completely different strategies exist:

Stemming: chop

Cut off endings with fixed rules. No dictionary, no grammar. Fast and crude.

Analogy: a gardener trimming a hedge. Quick, and sometimes too much comes off.

Lemmatization: look up

Find the dictionary headword (the lemma), using the word's grammar. Slower and accurate.

Analogy: looking the word up in a dictionary. You land on the headword, but you need to know if it is a noun or a verb.

Keep the two definitions parallel and brief. We now study each in turn: stemming first (simple rules), lemmatization second (dictionary plus grammar), and then compare them on the real dataset. Students should leave this slide knowing that one uses rules about letters, the other uses a dictionary about words.

Stemming from first principles: think like the stemmer

Suppose you must design a stemmer yourself. Look at the words and invent rules.

WordWhat you would doResult
catsRemove the final scat
walkingRemove ingwalk
runningRemove ing → runn. Two n's looks wrong. Remove a doubled consonantrun
singRemove ing → s. That is not a word! Add a rule: only strip ing if what remains still has a vowelsing
poniesRemove es → poni. Not a word, but it is fine if all forms (pony, ponies) land on the same stringponi

Key insight: a stem does not have to be a real word. It only has to be the same for every form of the word. That is what lets columns merge.

Ask students to propose each rule before you reveal it. This is exactly how Martin Porter built his algorithm in 1980: a list of rules, each with a condition about what remains of the word. The sing example introduces the condition idea: rules need conditions. Porter's conditions use the idea of "measure", roughly the count of vowel-consonant sequences in the stem.

The Porter stemmer: 8 steps, applied in order

The stemmer is just a pipeline in miniature. Each step has a short list of rules, and the first matching rule fires (if its condition holds).

StepWhat it handlesExamples
1aplural -s formscaresses → caress, ponies → poni, cats → cat
1b-ed and -ing (if a vowel remains), then tidy-upplastered → plaster, hopping → hop, sing stays sing
1cfinal y → i after a consonanthappy → happi
2long derivational suffixes become shorterrelational → relate, conditional → condition
3-icate, -ative, -ful, -nesstriplicate → triplic, hopeful → hope
4strip -al, -er, -ment, -ion ... only if the stem is long enoughcondition → condit, adjustment → adjust
5final tidy-up: drop e, ll → lrelate → relat, agree → agre, controll → control
Next: a simulator that shows which step changed the word, one step at a time. Try "relational", "happiness", "generalization".

No need to memorise the table. The idea is: suffix stripping happens in layers, from easy plural forms to long suffixes, and later steps work on the output of earlier ones. "relational" is a good example: step 2 turns it into "relate" and step 5 then drops the final e, giving "relat".

Note on measure: the condition "stem is long enough" is measured by the number of vowel-then-consonant sequences. "rel" has one, "relat" has two.

Type any English word or click an example. Rows appear only for steps that changed the word.

(3) happiness: only step 3 fires (ness removed, leaving happi) and happy: only step 1c fires (y to i). Different routes, same stem, which is the whole point. (4) generalization: steps 2, 3 and 4 fire one after another, finally "gener", a bad stem. (5) was: stem "wa" is a non-word. Ask what could go wrong.

Where stemming breaks: real output from NLTK

Over-stemming: different ideas merged

WordsStem
university, universe, universalunivers

Under-stemming and non-words

WordsStem

Two kinds of error. Over-stemming merges ideas that differ (a university is not a universe), so the model cannot tell them apart. Under-stemming fails to merge forms that should merge (ran, run). Fixed rules cannot know which is which: they only see letters.

All values here are real NLTK output (we ran them). "organization" and "organ" both become "organ": an organisation is not a body part, a classic over-stemming case.

The point: stemming is a blunt tool. For retrieval and bag of words it often works well enough. When meaning matters, we use lemmas.

Lemmatization: use a dictionary, and the part of speech

A lemma is the headword you would look up in a dictionary: the lemma of ran, runs, running is run.

Where does the dictionary come from?

WordNet: a large, hand-built English lexicon. It lists words, their base forms, and which are nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs. NLTK's WordNetLemmatizer reads it.

Why does it ask for a POS?

The same spelling can have different lemmas:

saw as a noun (the tool) → saw
saw as a verb (to see) → see

from nltk.stem import WordNetLemmatizer
wl = WordNetLemmatizer()
wl.lemmatize("saw")          # default pos is noun
wl.lemmatize("saw", pos="v") # 'v' = verb
saw
see

The POS argument uses single letters: n noun, v verb, a adjective, r adverb. If you do not pass it, NLTK assumes noun. This default is the single most common mistake in student code: they lemmatize a list of tokens without POS, then wonder why "running" stays "running".

Lemma lookup: change the part of speech, change the answer

Real results from NLTK's WordNet lemmatizer on words from our reviews. Compare with the Porter stem at the bottom.

Walk through: "better" as adjective is "good", as noun stays "better". "was" as verb becomes "be", but the default (noun) call turns it into the non-word "wa". "running" as a verb becomes "run", but as noun stays "running". "leaves": noun gives "leaf", verb gives "leave". Notice that the Porter stem is the same regardless of POS: it never asked.

Stop words: the most common words say the least

Words like theaofandisto appear in every text, so they do not help tell one review from another.

NLTK has a list

from nltk.corpus import stopwords
sw = set(stopwords.words("english"))
len(sw)       # 198

Why wrap in set(...)? A set answers "is this word in the list?" instantly. A list would check every item.

Remove when

topic classification, search, clustering with bag of words: the common words only add noise.

Keep when meaning depends on small words: sentiment ("not good"), question answering, translation, and most neural models. The list contains not, no, nor and "but".

Check the count live: the NLTK English stop word list has 198 entries in recent versions (older versions: 179). Run len() in the notebook so students see the true number on their machine.

The same steps in a different order give different results. Each row is a real mistake people make.

Common mistakes in this session

"Always remove stop words." Not for sentiment: "not good" must keep "not".

"Stems are words." They are not. "movi" is fine for merging columns, bad for showing to a user.

Lemmatizing without a POS. The default is noun, so verbs like "running" and "was" stay as they are.

Cleaning in the wrong order. Strip punctuation before tokenizing and "wasn't" cannot be split into "was" and "n't".

Judging by eye. "It looks cleaner" is not evidence. Count tokens and vocabulary before and after.

String functions on a list. After tokenizing, .lower() fails. Use a list comprehension.

Ask students which of these they have already done today. The first and the last two come up almost every time.

Recap, and the problem we are left with

  • Token = occurrence, vocabulary = distinct words. Vocabulary size becomes the width of the matrix.
  • Clean with small regex steps. Every step is a decision, and some destroy meaning ("10/10", ":)").
  • Tokenize: there is no single right answer. Split, regex, rule-based. Choose by data.
  • Stem = chop endings, fast, non-words. Lemmatize = dictionary + POS, real words.
  • On 2000 reviews the vocabulary went from 50,920 to 35,470 (stemming). Most of it is a long tail of rare words.
Still unsolved, and Session 3 starts here: the lemmatizer only works properly if it knows each word's part of speech. How does a computer decide that "running" is a verb here and an adjective there? And how can we find patterns like "adjective + noun" without a full grammar?

Run through the recap against the six objectives from slide 2. Then read the last box slowly. Session 3 opens by tagging a sentence and feeding the tags back into the lemmatizer.
