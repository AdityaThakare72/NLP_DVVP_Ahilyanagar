Before we write any code: what would the computer actually receive?

Make the problem real. Ask who has read online reviews before buying something. Nobody reads two thousand. The business value is in summarising them automatically. Then pose the question: what does the computer receive? Let two or three students guess. Most say "the words". The next slide shows that is not true.

What the computer actually receives

A computer has no idea what a word is. It stores numbers. Each character is one number from a fixed table (Unicode). Type anything and see.

The computer sees a long list of numbers. It does not see that g r e a t means "good". It also sees Great and great as different lists. Everything in NLP is about closing this gap.

Let students type their own name, a Marathi word, an emoji. Point out the capital G has a different number from small g (71 against 103). To the machine, Great and great are different. They will see this again in Session 2 when we lowercase.

Non-English scripts use more bytes per character in UTF-8 (the Devanagari letters take 3 bytes each). This returns on Day 3 when we discuss tokens and cost.

Why this is hard: three reasons

1. Ambiguity

One word, many meanings.

bank: river or money?
book: a thing, or to reserve?

2. Variation

One meaning, many spellings and styles.

good, gr8, goood, not bad all push towards "positive".

3. Context

Meaning depends on what is around the words.

"Great, another sequel." is not praise.

Plan: we cannot solve all three at once. So we split the job into small steps. That is a pipeline.

Do not rush. Take each card and ask the room for one more example, in English or in Marathi or Hindi. Marathi examples work well: the word "kal" can mean yesterday or tomorrow in Hindi and Marathi depending on tense context. That is ambiguity that humans resolve with context.

Ambiguity in action: "I saw her duck"

"duck" is a noun: a bird.

Isawherduck

I saw the duck that belongs to her. her = possessive ("her duck"), duck = noun.

"duck" is a verb: bend down.

Isawherduck

I saw her bend down. her = the person, duck = verb.

Same four words, same order, two meanings. The only way to choose is to look at grammar and context. A model that ignores them will guess. We will see how tagging fixes this in Session 3.

Click both tabs. Ask: which reading did you get first? Why? Usually the bird. Now ask what extra sentence would force the other meaning ("I saw her duck under the table"). Context decides.

The first-principles idea: do it in small steps

Nobody solves a hard problem in one jump. We split it so that each step does one small thing and hands its result to the next step.

Making tea. Boil water, add leaves, wait, strain, add milk. Each step needs the output of the one before. If you pour milk before boiling, the later steps cannot fix it.

Same in NLP

Raw text → cleaned text → tokens → base words → tags → numbers → model.

Mistakes flow downstream. If cleaning deletes the word "not", no later step can bring it back. Always look at what a step did before you move on.

The tea analogy lands well. The key lesson is the last box: errors flow forward. This is why the notebook inspects the data after every step. If a student asks why we look at five sample rows after each step, this is the answer.

A very common beginner error: calling a string function on a list, or a list function on a string. text.lower() works on a str. After tokenizing you have a list, so tokens.lower() fails with AttributeError. The fix is not a trick, it is knowing which stage you are at.

Why mistakes flow downstream: a live example

Pretend a model has to decide whether the review is positive or negative. Tick the boxes and see what the model is left with.

Choose sentence 2, "this is not a bad film". Turn on stop word removal and the model sees only "bad film". A review that says it is good now looks bad. Then tick "keep negations" and show it is fixed. The point: a preprocessing choice is a modelling choice. There is no free step.

Honest caveat to share: whether stop word removal helps depends on the model. For deep models it is usually left out. For bag of words models on small data it often helps. We will test this on real data in Session 2.

Language has layers: from letters to intent

To understand a sentence we need more than the words. Linguists describe five layers, each built on the one below.

LevelQuestionExample
1. Words (lexical, morphology)What are the words and their parts?"un-happi-ness" = un + happy + ness
2. Word classes (POS)Is it a noun, verb, adjective?"book" is a noun in "read a book", a verb in "book a ticket"
3. Grammar (syntax)How do words combine?"dog bites man" and "man bites dog" use the same words
4. Meaning (semantics)What does it say?"bank" = river edge or money place
5. Intent (pragmatics)What does the speaker want?"Can you pass the salt?" is a request, not a question about ability

Go one row at a time and ask students to give another example before you reveal ours. Then say: every NLP task you have heard of lives at one of these levels. Stemming is level 1. POS tagging is level 2. Parsing is level 3. Word sense disambiguation and sentiment are level 4. Dialogue and question answering touch level 5.

The whole of Day 1 climbs levels 1 to 3 with some help for 4. Day 2 and Day 3 reach for 4 and 5.

Same sentence, ambiguous at different levels

A computer needs rules for each level because each level can be ambiguous in its own way. Pick a tab.

"I went to the bank."

Is it a river bank or a money bank? The word alone does not tell you. You need the neighbouring words: "to deposit a cheque" or "to fish".

Tool in this workshop: embeddings (Day 2), contextual models (Day 3).

"Time flies like an arrow"

"flies" can be a verb (time moves fast) or a noun ("time flies" as insects, like an arrow). "like" can be a verb or a preposition. The tagger must pick one reading.

Tool: POS tagger, Session 3.

"I saw the man with the telescope."

Reading A: I used a telescope to see him. Reading B: the man had a telescope. Same words, same tags, two different trees. The grammar allows both.

Tool: parser, Session 3 and spaCy dependency parse.

"The movie was sick."

Positive slang or something negative? Grammar is fine. The meaning depends on who says it and when.

Tool: sentiment models, Day 2 and 3.

"Great, another delay."

Literally positive. Actually sarcastic. The surface words say one thing, the speaker means the opposite.

Tool: large language models, Day 3. Still hard.

Do not go deep. The goal is to make students feel that each of these is a different kind of difficulty and that no single trick handles all five. The muted lines say which session handles each. Sarcasm is a good place to be honest: even the best models get it wrong often.

Two toolkits, two philosophies

NLTK: the toolbox

Natural Language Toolkit. Built for teaching. Dozens of small, separate tools: you pick the tokenizer, pick the stemmer, pick the tagger. Everything is visible and swappable.

Good for: learning how each step works, trying alternatives, built-in corpora.

spaCy: the appliance

Built for production. You load one pipeline, hand it text, and get a fully analysed document with tokens, tags, parse and entities, all at once, fast.

Good for: real applications, speed, ready-trained models.

NLTK is a kitchen with every knife, pan and spice on the wall, you cook step by step. spaCy is a microwave meal: press one button, it is ready, but you do not see inside.

We learn with NLTK so you see each step. We use spaCy where one-line, accurate output matters. In industry you will meet both.

Students sometimes think they must choose one forever. Reassure them: NLTK for understanding, spaCy for shipping. In this workshop NLTK is the default for Sessions 2 to 5, and spaCy appears in Session 1 (to compare), Session 3 (named entities, dependency parse) and Day 3.

NLTK in 4 lines, read line by line

# 1. import the module and the tokenizer function we need
import nltk
from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize

# 2. a plain Python string. Nothing NLP-specific yet
text = "Wow, this movie wasn't good at all!"

# 3. word_tokenize cuts the string into a list of tokens
tokens = word_tokenize(text)
print(tokens)
['Wow', ',', 'this', 'movie', 'was', "n't", 'good', 'at', 'all', '!']

Read the output

A list of 10 strings. Punctuation became its own tokens. "wasn't" was split into was and n't: the negation is a separate piece of meaning.

What NLTK did not do

It did not lowercase ("Wow" kept its capital), did not remove punctuation, did not tag anything. You add each step yourself.

Walk through the code in three comment blocks. Point at each comment. The key ideas: word_tokenize is a function, it takes a string, it returns a list. Nothing is hidden. Then read the output: count the tokens with the class (10), spot the n't.

In the notebook the same call is run on a real review and we print len(tokens) and the first 25 tokens.

spaCy in 4 lines: one call, a rich object

# 1. load a trained English pipeline (small version, about 12 MB)
import spacy
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

# 2. calling nlp on a string runs the WHOLE pipeline
doc = nlp("Wow, this movie wasn't good at all!")

# 3. doc is a sequence of Token objects, each with many attributes
for token in doc:
    print(token.text, token.lemma_, token.pos_)

What nlp(...) did silently

Tokenised, tagged each word, found base forms, parsed grammar, spotted entities. Five steps in one call.

Why token.lemma_ has an underscore

In spaCy, token.pos is an integer id (a number the library uses inside). The version with an underscore, token.pos_, is the readable string. Almost always you want the underscore.

Two traps for beginners here. First: the underscore. If you print token.pos you get a number like 94 and it looks broken. Second: nlp is called on the whole string, not a list of tokens, because the tokenizer is inside the pipeline.

Next slide lets students click through every token and see the attributes.

Click a token: what spaCy knows about each word

Real output from en_core_web_sm. Compare "was" and "n't", "movie" and "wasn't".

Let students pick tokens. Good ones: "n't" has lemma "not", so spaCy normalises the negation for you. "good" has dep acomp (adjectival complement) and head "was": the parse says good describes the subject through "was". Ask: which attributes did NLTK give us? Only the token text.

Do not explain dependency labels in depth yet. That is Session 3. Just say that head and dep tell us the grammar tree.

There is no single correct tokenisation. .split() glues punctuation to words ("all," and "dry!!!" become strange tokens). Different tools count different things, so any count (vocabulary size, tokens per review) depends on the tool and the cleaning you applied before it.

The counts come from the actual run on the sentence we used in the explorer. Ask: which is right? The honest answer is "the one that suits your task". For sentiment, the n't piece matters; for search, you may want "wasn't" kept whole.

Meet the dataset: 2000 movie reviews as a table

Before any step, we load the data and look at it. A table (pandas DataFrame) gives one row per review.

import pandas as pd
from nltk.corpus import movie_reviews

# fileids() lists the 2000 review files, e.g. 'neg/cv000_29416.txt'
rows = []
for fid in movie_reviews.fileids():
    rows.append({
        "id":    fid.split("/")[1][:-4],        # 'cv000_29416'
        "label": movie_reviews.categories(fid)[0],  # 'neg' or 'pos'
        "text":  movie_reviews.raw(fid),
    })
df = pd.DataFrame(rows)

Why build a list of dictionaries first, and then the DataFrame? Because appending a row to a DataFrame one by one is slow and awkward. Collecting plain Python dicts, then building the table once, is the standard pattern. Each dict becomes a row, each key becomes a column.

This is the exact load cell of every notebook in the workshop. Explain the loop line by line: fileids gives file names, each name starts with the folder pos or neg which is the label, raw reads the text.

The answer to the ask box is revealed on click. Students often want to write df.append in a loop. Mention that df.append was removed in pandas 2.

Facts we can read here

df.shape is (2000, 5). Labels: 1000 neg and 1000 pos, perfectly balanced. A review has on average 746 words, the shortest has 17 and the longest 2678.

Something to notice in the raw text

The text starts: plot : two teen couples go to a church party , drink and then drive . Punctuation is already separated by spaces and everything is lowercase. This corpus is pre-tokenised. Real-world text will not be this tidy.

The numbers are real. Ask the group what the shape (2000, 5) means: rows then columns. Why do we compute n_words? Because the length of a review will matter later: longer reviews have bigger count vectors.

The pre-tokenised point is easy to miss and important. It means word_tokenize on this data does less work than it would on a tweet. In Session 2 we also practise on the three hand-written messy sentences.

Common mistakes in this session

"The computer reads text like I do." It receives numbers for characters. Everything else is a rule or a learned pattern.

"More preprocessing is always better." Every step can destroy information (the removed "not"). Inspect each one.

"spaCy is better than NLTK." Different goals. One shows you the steps, the other ships the result.

"Splitting on spaces is tokenisation." It glues punctuation to words and misses "wasn't" as two pieces.

Ask students which of these they believed before today. Be honest that all four are commonly believed by smart beginners.

Recap, and the problem we are left with

  • Computers see characters as numbers. An NLP pipeline turns text into numbers in small steps.
  • Seven stages: raw, clean, tokenize, normalise, structure, fix words, vectorise. Each changes the data type.
  • Language has five levels. Each level is ambiguous in its own way.
  • NLTK shows each step. spaCy does many at once.
  • We loaded 2000 reviews as a DataFrame and learned to inspect before trusting.
Still unsolved, and Session 2 starts here: the reviews are full of noise (case, punctuation, "run", "running", "ran") and we have only seen how one tokenizer cuts a sentence. How do we clean the text and decide what a "word" is, without destroying meaning?
