Predicting the Next Word: N-gram Language Models

Given "the special", how likely is "effects" next? Counting answers that, and it is the oldest ancestor of today's large language models.

Where we are: the mistakes were about order

From Session 2: the classifier's worst errors came from praise for other films, verdicts after long summaries, and "not" before "good". Adding word pairs as columns helped only a little (85.8% to 87.2%).

What a bag of words knows

Which words occur, and how often.

{good: 1, not: 1, was: 1, the: 1, movie: 1}

What we want to know

Which word follows which. That "the movie was not good" is likely English, and "good not was movie the" is not.

Today: a model that gives a probability to every sequence of words.

The classifier and the language model ask different questions: "which label?" versus "how likely is this text?". Both are built by counting.

What is a language model?

A model that answers: how likely is this next word, given the words before it? Multiply those answers and you get the probability of a whole sentence.

From words to sentences: the chain rule

The probability of a sentence is the probability of each word given everything before it, multiplied.

P(the movie was long) = P(the) × P(movie | the) × P(was | the movie) × P(long | the movie was)

The problem: to estimate P(long | the movie was) we count how often "the movie was" is followed by "long". For a 20-word sentence the last term needs a 19-word history. Almost every 19-word history occurs never, even in millions of reviews.

So the chain rule is exact but useless as it stands. We need to forget most of the history.

The chain rule is not an assumption; it is always true. The approximation comes next. Compare with Naive Bayes in Session 1: there we assumed words independent of each other entirely. Here we keep a little dependence.

The Markov assumption: remember only the last word or two

Someone joins a conversation late and has only heard the last word or two. They can still guess the next word fairly well most of the time, and sometimes badly.

From Day 1, Session 3: a finite automaton has states that remember a little. A bigram model is a machine whose state is "the last word", with a probability on every arrow.

Named after Andrey Markov, who in 1913 counted sequences of vowels and consonants in Pushkin's Eugene Onegin by hand. An n-gram model uses n minus 1 words of history. The link to automata is exact: a bigram model is a probabilistic finite-state machine. Session 4 builds on that view.

Estimating a bigram: count and divide

P(w | v) = count(v w) / count(v)

This is the maximum likelihood estimate (MLE): the numbers that make the training text as likely as possible. In plain words: the share of times v was followed by w.

"special" is followed by something 452 times in training; 307 of those are "effects": P(effects | special) = 307 / 452 = 0.68.
A trigram looks further: "the special" → effects 100 times of 106: P(effects | the special) = 0.94.

More history, sharper predictions, when the history has been seen. That condition is the whole story of this session.

The 452 is the number of times "special" appears followed by any token in the training text, including punctuation. "the special" occurred 106 times. These shares are computed from the 1600 training reviews. Mention that we add the markers start-of-sentence and end-of-sentence so the first word and the end are also predicted (next slide).

P(<s> i liked the plot </s>) = 2/3 × 1 × 1 × 1/3 × 1 = 2/9 = 0.22

Now "the plot was long", a perfectly good sentence: P(was | plot) = 0/1 = 0. The whole sentence gets probability 0, because "plot was" never appeared.

"the" is followed by something 3 times: movie twice, plot once. So P(plot | the) = 1/3. Do the second sentence with the class: P(the | start) = 1/3, P(plot | the) = 1/3, P(was | plot) = 0. Zero. Same disease as Naive Bayes in Session 1, same cure on the next slides. The simulator on the next slide uses exactly this corpus.

Let each model write: unigram, bigram, trigram

Unigram: a random bag of review words, no order at all. Bigram: every adjacent pair is plausible, the sentence is not. The zero problem is everywhere in real text

19.3% of the word pairs in the test reviews never occur in the 1.2 million training words.

55.8% of the word triples in the test reviews never occur in training.

With plain counting, almost every test sentence contains at least one unseen pair, so almost every test sentence gets probability 0. The model calls normal English impossible.

Language is creative: most sentences you read today were never written before. A model must keep some probability for things it has not seen.

Measured on the 400 test reviews against counts from the 1600 training reviews. The jump from 19 to 56 percent is the price of the extra word of history: there are vastly more possible triples than pairs.

Smoothing 1: add one (or add k) to every count

P(w | v) = (count(v w) + k) / (count(v) + k × V)

Toy corpus, k = 1, V = 8

P(was | plot) = (0 + 1) / (1 + 8) = 0.11. Not zero any more.
P(plot | the) = (1 + 1) / (3 + 8) = 0.18. Was 0.33.

Add-one steals too much

With 25,543 possible next words, adding 1 to each gives 25,543 imaginary counts to every context. A context seen 5 times now has 99.98% of its probability on things never seen.

From Session 1: this is Laplace smoothing again, the α of Naive Bayes. Same formula, same reason. A small k (0.01) is usually far better than 1.

V is the number of possible next words: the vocabulary plus the end marker. 5 real counts against 25,543 imaginary ones: 25543 over 25548 is 99.98 percent. That is why add-one makes the bigram model worse than the unigram one in the real results two slides later.

Smoothing 2: mix models of different lengths

When the trigram has never been seen, fall back on the bigram; when that is thin, on the unigram. Interpolation always blends all three:

P(w | u v) = λ1 P(w) + λ2 P(w | v) + λ3 P(w | u v)        λ1 + λ2 + λ3 = 1

How to choose the λs

Keep 10% of the training sentences aside, try combinations, keep the one that predicts the held-out text best. Never the test set (Session 2).

What we found

λ = 0.3 unigram, 0.5 bigram, 0.2 trigram. Most weight on the bigram: it is seen often enough to trust and still has context.

Better smoothing methods (Kneser-Ney) were the state of the art until neural networks. Same goal: share probability sensibly between seen and unseen.

Backoff is the cousin of interpolation: use the trigram if seen, otherwise the bigram, otherwise the unigram, with discounting so probabilities still sum to 1. Interpolation is simpler to explain and code. The lambdas were chosen by a grid search on 10 percent of the training sentences.

Measuring a language model: perplexity

A good model gives high probability to real text it has not seen. Perplexity turns that into a number you can compare.

perplexity = P(test words) -1/N   (N = number of predicted words)

Intuition: how many words is the model choosing between, on average? A fair die: every face has probability 1/6, perplexity 6. A model with perplexity 200 is as unsure as if it were choosing uniformly among 200 words at each step. Lower is better.

Toy corpus: P(i liked the plot) = 2/9 over 5 predictions (4 words and </s>). Perplexity = (9/2)1/5 = 1.35. This tiny model is rarely unsure on its own training sentences.

Perplexity is 1 over the geometric mean of the per-word probabilities. Taking the N-th root makes it independent of length, so long and short test sets can be compared. Only compare perplexities computed on the same test text with the same vocabulary; otherwise the numbers are not comparable.

Words the model has never seen: <unk>

From Day 1, Session 4: new text always contains words not in the vocabulary. A language model must predict them too.

The trick

In training, replace every word seen only once by the token <unk>. The model learns how often "some rare word" appears and where. At test time, every unknown word becomes <unk>.

Our numbers

Vocabulary: 25,543 words seen at least twice. 3.0% of test tokens become <unk>. "the <unk>" (1,845 times) is more common than "the movie" (1,557).

A trap: a model with a tiny vocabulary maps many words to <unk> and predicts <unk> easily, so its perplexity looks great. Compare perplexities only with the same vocabulary.

The threshold (count at least 2) is a choice. With a threshold of 100 the vocabulary would be tiny and perplexity would drop, but the model would be useless. This is the same idea as min_df in Day 1, Session 5.

Where n-gram models break

Short memory

"The movies that the director made after his first big hit are..." The verb agrees with a word 9 positions back. A trigram sees only "big hit".

Sparsity explodes

25,543 words: 650 million possible pairs, 16 trillion triples. Most are never seen. Each extra word of context makes it worse.

No notion of similar words

Having seen "great acting", the model knows nothing about "excellent acting". Every word is a separate symbol.

Day 3 answers all three

Neural language models represent words as vectors (similar words, similar vectors) and use attention to look back as far as needed. Same task, predict the next word.

The three limits map onto the three big ideas of Day 3: word vectors, attention, and pretraining on huge text. Large language models are trained on exactly the objective of this session: predict the next word.

Common mistakes in this session

Forgetting <s> and </s>. The first word and the sentence end are then never predicted.

Dividing by the total word count for a bigram. Divide by the count of the previous word.

Add-one by default. On a real vocabulary it ruins the model. Use small k or interpolation.

Multiplying probabilities. Underflow again. Sum logs, as in Session 1.

Comparing perplexity across vocabularies. Smaller vocabulary, more <unk>, fake improvement.

Tuning λ or k on the test set. Use held-out training sentences.

The second mistake is the most common one in student code: P(w | v) needs the count of v as a first word in a pair, not the size of the corpus.

Recap, and the problem we are left with

  • A language model gives a probability to the next word, and so to any sentence.
  • The Markov assumption keeps only 1 or 2 words of history; probabilities are counts divided by counts.
  • Unseen pairs and triples are everywhere: smooth, preferably by interpolation. Map rare words to <unk>.
  • Perplexity = how many words the model is effectively choosing between. Lower is better, on the same test text and vocabulary.
Still unsolved, and Session 4 starts here: we predicted the words. In Day 1, Session 3 a tagger predicted a hidden label for every word (noun, verb...), and we said it uses "odds from the word, adjusted by the neighbours". How exactly? Combine a model of which tag follows which (a bigram model over tags) with how likely each word is for each tag. That is a Hidden Markov Model.

Session 4 reuses everything from today: counts divided by counts, start and end markers, smoothing, logs. The new piece is the Viterbi algorithm, which finds the best tag sequence without trying all of them.
