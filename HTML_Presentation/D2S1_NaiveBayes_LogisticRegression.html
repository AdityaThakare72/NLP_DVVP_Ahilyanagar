Learning from Counts: Naive Bayes and Logistic Regression

We have a matrix of word counts and a label for every review. How does a program learn to predict the label of a review it has never seen?

Day 2 · Session 1 of 5 · statistical NLP and machine learning

Day 1 ended with a matrix X (2000 reviews by 39,659 words) and labels y. Today is about learning from data instead of writing rules. This first session builds two classifiers from first principles: Naive Bayes, which counts, and logistic regression, which adjusts weights. Both are still the standard baselines in industry, and both are fully inspectable, which is why we start here before any neural network.

By the end of this session you can...

  1. Explain what it means for a program to learn from examples, and why we hold back a test set
  2. Compute a Naive Bayes prediction by hand, including smoothing and why we use logs
  3. Explain logistic regression as a weighted vote of words passed through a sigmoid, and how the weights are learned
  4. Train both in scikit-learn, read every parameter, and inspect what they learned
  5. Say for one review why the model chose its label, and where each model breaks

Objective 5 is the one people skip in practice. A model that cannot be explained cannot be trusted. Both of today's models can be explained word by word, and we will do exactly that.

First attempt: write the rule ourselves

Count a few positive and negative words. More positive words: say pos.

positive = ["great", "best", "excellent", "wonderful", "perfect"]
negative = ["bad", "worst", "boring", "stupid", "waste"]

def rule(review):
    words = review.split()
    p = sum(words.count(w) for w in positive)
    n = sum(words.count(w) for w in negative)
    return "pos" if p > n else "neg"

Accuracy on all 2000 reviews: 68.4%

527 reviews are ties (often 0 against 0): the rule has nothing to say. And who decided these ten words, and why are they all worth the same?

This is a real result on the 2000 reviews. 68 percent is better than a coin (50 percent), so the words do carry signal. The two problems lead to the two models. Problem 1: we chose the words. Learning lets the data choose among all 39,659. Problem 2: every word counts the same. Learning gives each word its own strength.

What "learning" means: rules from examples

Training

Show the program reviews with their labels. It adjusts its own numbers (word strengths) so that its answers match the labels.

Testing

Show it reviews it has never seen, hide the labels, and compare its answers with the truth. Only this score tells us how it will do on new reviews.

A student who memorises last year's exam answers scores 100% on last year's exam. The real test is a new exam. We split our reviews: 1600 to train on, 400 locked away for the test.

From Day 1, Session 5: the vectorizer is fitted on the training reviews only, then used to transform the test reviews. Otherwise the test is not a surprise.

The 80/20 split with random_state 42 and stratification is the one used all day. Stratify means both parts keep the 50/50 balance of labels. Session 2 asks whether a single split can be trusted.

Naive Bayes, idea 1: how likely is each word in each class?

Read all positive training reviews and count words. Do the same for negative. Turn counts into shares.

wordcount in pos reviewscount in neg reviewsreading
bad290833almost 3 times as common in negative reviews
worst39215a strong negative signal
perfect19873a positive signal
good1,002934nearly even: people write "not good" and "good, but"
not2,3872,133nearly even on its own

P(word | class) = count of the word in that class / total words in that class. Read: "if I pick a random word from a positive review, how likely is it to be bad?"

Counts are from the 1600 training reviews. "not" is interesting: as a single word it carries almost no information about the label, even though negation obviously matters. The meaning lives in the pair "not good", which a single-word model cannot see. This becomes the handoff at the end of Session 2.

Positive reviews are a little longer on average, so raw counts favour pos slightly; dividing by the total words per class corrects for that.

Naive Bayes, idea 2: turn it around with Bayes' rule

We know P(words | class). We want P(class | words). Bayes' rule flips it.

P(class | review)  ∝  P(class) × P(review | class)

A doctor hears a cough. Prior: how common is each illness at all (cold is common, pneumonia rare)? Likelihood: how often does each illness cause a cough? Multiply, compare. A common illness that often causes coughs wins.

Prior P(class)

Share of training reviews in the class. Here 800 of 1600 are pos: 0.5 each.

Likelihood P(review | class)

How likely is this exact bag of words if the review were positive? Needs idea 3.

∝ means "proportional to": we skip the division by P(review) because it is the same for both classes and does not change which one is bigger.

Write the full rule on the board if students know it: P(c | d) = P(c) P(d | c) / P(d). Then explain that P(d) is the same for both classes, so only the top matters for choosing. At the end we normalise the two numbers to sum to 1 to get a probability.

Naive Bayes, idea 3: the "naive" assumption

Pretend every word was picked independently. Then the likelihood of the whole review is just the product of the word likelihoods.

P(review | pos) = P(w1 | pos) × P(w2 | pos) × ... × P(wn | pos)

This is plainly false. "special" makes "effects" very likely; "not" changes "good". The model ignores all of that.

Why use it anyway? Without it we would need the probability of every possible combination of words, which no amount of data can give. With it we need one number per word per class: easy to count, fast, and it works surprisingly well for choosing a label.

Naive is a technical term here, not an insult. The assumption makes the model wrong about probabilities (it is usually far too confident) but often right about which class is bigger. We will see the overconfidence in the next session when we look at the predicted probabilities.

The zero problem, and the fix: smoothing

Without smoothing: "bad" never appears in the positive training reviews, so P(bad | pos) = 0/7 = 0. One zero in a product makes the whole product 0. A single word makes "pos" impossible, no matter how many positive words the review has.

Laplace (add-one) smoothing: pretend every vocabulary word was seen once more in every class.
P(w | class) = (count + α) / (total words in class + α × vocabulary size)
With α = 1: (0 + 1) / (7 + 8) = 1/15. Small, but not zero.

Why add α × vocabulary to the bottom? We added α to each of the 8 words, so the total grew by 8α. The shares still add up to 1.

This returns in Session 3: a language model has the same zero problem for word pairs it has never seen, and the same fix.

The parameter is alpha in scikit-learn's MultinomialNB, default 1.0. The next slide's simulator has an alpha slider: set it to 0 and the test review becomes impossible for pos. On the real data a very large alpha flattens all differences between words: alpha 10 dropped test accuracy to 74.5 percent; values between 0.5 and 5 all gave about 82 percent.

A test word not in the training vocabulary is simply ignored, which is what scikit-learn does too (the vectorizer has no column for it). Add "pos: bad but great" to the training lines and watch P(bad | pos) rise and the prediction flip.

Real reviews have 700 words: multiply 700 small numbers?

Underflow

Each P(word | class) is around 0.001 or smaller. 700 of them multiplied give about 10-2100. A computer's smallest number is about 10-308. The product becomes exactly 0 for both classes.

Fix: add logs instead

log(a × b) = log a + log b. So we add logarithms:
score(pos) = log P(pos) + Σ log P(w | pos)
The class with the larger score wins. Same answer, no underflow.

A bonus for explaining: the difference log P(w | pos) - log P(w | neg) is one number per word: how hard that word pushes towards pos (positive) or neg (negative). A review's decision is the sum of its words' pushes.

Logs of numbers below 1 are negative, which confuses some students. Only the comparison between the two class scores matters. The log ratio per word is what the "why this label" simulator shows later. scikit-learn stores the logs in feature_log_prob_.

Naive Bayes in scikit-learn, line by line

from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import CountVectorizer
from sklearn.naive_bayes import MultinomialNB

train, test = train_test_split(df, test_size=0.2, random_state=42, stratify=df["label"])

vec = CountVectorizer()
X_train = vec.fit_transform(train["text"])   # learn vocabulary on train only
X_test  = vec.transform(test["text"])        # reuse it on test

nb = MultinomialNB(alpha=1.0)                  # alpha = the smoothing from the last slides
nb.fit(X_train, train["label"])               # counting: one pass over the training matrix
nb.score(X_test, test["label"])                # share of test reviews predicted correctly

Multinomial

The model treats a review as word counts drawn from a bag: exactly our worked example.

fit

Counts words per class, adds α, stores the logs. Took 5 ms on 1600 reviews.

Result

81.8% correct on the 400 test reviews. The hand rule had 68.4%.

Other useful pieces: nb.predict(X) gives labels, nb.predict_proba(X) gives the two probabilities, nb.classes_ shows the order of the classes, nb.feature_log_prob_ holds log P(word | class) with shape (2, vocabulary). The 5 milliseconds is a measured time, and it matters: Naive Bayes trains in one pass, so it scales to millions of documents.

What did Naive Bayes learn? Read its strongest words

Words used at least 150 times in training, ranked by log P(w | pos) - log P(w | neg). Rounded.

Even here, names sneak in (jackie, batman, harry, disney). Lower the threshold to 20 uses and the top words become names: mulan, flynt, shrek (only in positive reviews), seagal, magoo, jawbreaker (only in negative). Even nbsp, a web formatting leftover, is a top negative word. Naive Bayes trusts any word that happens to fall on one side.

These are the real strongest words. The second list is a warning: rare words that happen to appear only in one class get extreme ratios. A movie name tells you about this dataset, not about sentiment, and will not help on next year's reviews. This is overfitting, in its simplest visible form. nbsp is HTML for a non-breaking space; it survived in a few reviews scraped from the web, all negative, by chance.

A different idea: a weighted vote of words

Give every word a weight. Positive weight pushes towards pos, negative towards neg. Add up weight × count, plus a starting value b.

z = b + wbad × count(bad) + wgreat × count(great) + ...

Real weights learned from the 1600 reviews: wbad = -0.76, wgreat = +0.38, wworst = -0.52.
A review with "bad" twice and "great" once (all else ignored): z = 2 × (-0.76) + 0.38 = -1.14, leaning negative.

Same as the hand rule?

The hand rule was this formula with weights +1 and -1 for ten words, 0 for all others. Now the data chooses every weight.

But z is any number

-1.14 is not a probability. We need to squash it into 0 to 1.

The link back to the hand-written rule is the key insight: logistic regression is that rule with learned weights. The intercept b is the score of an empty review. Here it is -1.08, not 0, even though the classes are balanced: b and the word weights are learned together, and b absorbs whatever overall offset the weights leave.

The sigmoid: turn any score into a probability

p = 1 / (1 + e-z). Drag z. Where is p exactly 0.5? What happens to big positive and big negative z?

z = 0 gives 0.5: the model has no preference. z = 2 gives 0.88, z = -1.14 from the previous slide gives 0.24. The curve never reaches 0 or 1, so the model is never completely certain. The 0.5 line is the default decision threshold; Session 2 moves it.

How are the weights learned? Walk downhill

  1. Start with every weight 0. Every review gets z = 0, so p = 0.5: a coin.
  2. Measure how wrong the model is: for each review, the log loss is -log(probability given to the true label). Confident and right: near 0. Confident and wrong: large.
  3. Find the direction: for each weight, would the loss go down if we increased it a little, or decreased it? The error (p - true label) times the word's count tells us.
  4. Take a small step in that direction for every weight at once.
  5. Repeat until the loss stops falling.

Walking down a hill in fog: you cannot see the bottom, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. Step that way, feel again. This is gradient descent.

Do not show the derivative unless the class asks. The update for each weight is: weight minus learning rate times the average of (p minus y) times the feature value. If the model said 0.9 for a negative review that contains "bad" three times, the error is 0.9, so the weight of bad goes down by a lot. scikit-learn uses a faster method than plain steps (lbfgs), but the idea is the same.

Train it yourself: two real features, 2000 real reviews

Each review is reduced to two numbers: how many of the 5 positive words, how many of the 5 negative words. Try to beat the hand rule (68.4%) with the sliders, then press "50 steps".

With all weights at 0 the accuracy is 50 percent and the loss is 0.693 (that is -log 0.5). Each "50 steps" lowers the loss: w_pos becomes positive, w_neg negative, and the yellow line separates the green cloud from the red one as well as a straight line can. After a few hundred steps it settles at about 70 percent (scikit-learn on the same two features gives 69.9 percent), a little better than the hand rule because it learned that the negative words are stronger than the positive ones. Many points sit at (0,0) and cannot be separated at all: that is the 527 ties from the hand rule. Real logistic regression does the same with 39,659 features instead of 2, which is why it reaches 85 percent.

Regularisation: stop the weights from memorising

With 39,659 words and only 1600 reviews, the model could give huge weights to rare words (mulan, seagal) and fit the training set perfectly. A penalty on large weights stops that.

The knob: C

In scikit-learn, LogisticRegression(C=1.0). Small C = strong penalty, small weights, a cautious model. Large C = weak penalty, the model trusts the training data more.

Answer to a Day 1 puzzle: TF-IDF lost to counts in the Day 1 scoreboard. TF-IDF rows have length 1, so their values are tiny, and the default C=1 kept the weights too small. With C=100, TF-IDF reaches 85.0%. Defaults are not neutral.

Accuracies are 5-fold cross-validated on the 2000 reviews, the same folds as the Day 1 scoreboard. Counts barely change with C; TF-IDF changes by 7 points. This closes the hypothesis we flagged on Day 1 without claiming more than the numbers show: tuning C is part of comparing representations fairly. Session 2 explains how to tune it without cheating, with a validation step inside the training data.

Logistic regression in scikit-learn, line by line

from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

lr = LogisticRegression(C=1.0, max_iter=3000)
lr.fit(X_train, train["label"])            # many downhill steps
lr.score(X_test, test["label"])             # accuracy on the 400 test reviews

weights = lr.coef_[0]                         # one weight per vocabulary word
lr.intercept_                                  # the starting value b
lr.predict_proba(X_test)[:5]                 # [P(neg), P(pos)] for 5 reviews

C

Penalty strength, inverted: larger C, weaker penalty. Default 1.0.

max_iter

The most downhill steps allowed. If it stops early you get a ConvergenceWarning; raise it.

Result

85.2% on the same 400 test reviews (NB: 81.8%). Training took about 2 s, NB 5 ms.

coef_ has shape (1, vocabulary) for two classes, hence the [0]. The class order is in lr.classes_, alphabetical: neg then pos, so positive weights push towards pos. The training time is measured on this machine and varies, but the gap of several hundred times is typical.

What did logistic regression learn?

Different from Naive Bayes: the top words are common, general words (bad, worst, nothing, unfortunately, great, fun). The penalty makes rare names expensive, so it relies on words that appear in many reviews. "plot" and "script" are negative: people mention the plot when it fails.

Some weights surprise students: "seen" and "back" are positive, "only" and "women" negative. Ask why. Plausible stories exist ("one of the best I have seen", "only redeeming feature"), but they are guesses. The model found a correlation in 1600 reviews, not a rule of English. The honest answer is: look at reviews containing the word before telling a story.

Why this label? Every word's push, for one review

Green words push towards pos, red towards neg; darker is stronger. Switch between the two models. Look for the review the model gets wrong, and why.

These are short reviews from the test set, real predictions. For logistic regression each word's push is its weight times one occurrence; the sum plus b is z. For Naive Bayes it is the log ratio. The wrong review is the interesting one: read it aloud and find what the model missed (sarcasm, contrast with "but", a negative review that uses many normal positive words). This leads straight to the next session: one accuracy number hides these cases.

Avoid declaring a winner on 2 points: Session 2 explains why. The double-counting row is the main theoretical difference: naive independence means two words that always appear together both add their full push, while logistic regression learns them jointly. Both are linear models: each word has one fixed push, regardless of context. Neither can understand "not good" as a unit.

What the practical does

  1. Load the DataFrame, run the hand rule, and measure its accuracy and its ties
  2. Rebuild the worked example: compute Naive Bayes by hand with a loop, check it against MultinomialNB
  3. Split 80/20, vectorize correctly, train Naive Bayes, inspect feature_log_prob_ and the strongest words at two thresholds
  4. Show underflow: multiply real probabilities for one review, then add logs
  5. Train logistic regression, read coef_, and compute z and p for one review by hand from the weights
  6. Sweep alpha and C and put the results in a table
  7. For three test reviews, list the words that pushed the decision, and read one wrong prediction

Step 5 is the most valuable: students compute the model's output with their own arithmetic from the learned weights and get the same probability as predict_proba. That removes the black box.

Common mistakes in this session

Testing on the training data. Training accuracy is close to 100% for logistic regression. It says nothing.

alpha = 0. One unseen word in a class makes it impossible.

Multiplying probabilities. Underflow to 0. Work in logs.

Telling stories about weights. "women is negative" is a correlation in 1600 reviews, not a fact.

Ignoring ConvergenceWarning. The weights are not finished; raise max_iter.

Comparing models with default settings only. TF-IDF looked worse until C was tuned.

Training accuracy: in the notebook we print it on purpose so students see the 100 percent and learn to distrust it.

Recap, and the problem we are left with

  • Learning = adjusting numbers to match labelled examples, then testing on held-back examples.
  • Naive Bayes: prior × product of word likelihoods, with smoothing for zeros and logs for underflow. Fast, but trusts rare words.
  • Logistic regression: weighted vote of words, squashed by a sigmoid, weights learned by walking downhill on the log loss. C controls how cautious.
  • Both can be explained word by word. Neither sees word order.
Still unsolved, and Session 2 starts here: logistic regression is "85.2% accurate". On which 400 reviews? Would another split give 80% or 90%? Is it worse at catching negative reviews than positive ones? What if only 1 review in 10 were negative? One number hides all of this.

The handoff: an accuracy figure without context. Session 2 opens it up into a confusion matrix, precision and recall, thresholds and cross-validation.
