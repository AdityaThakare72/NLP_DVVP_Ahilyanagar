Where we are

From Session 3: fine-tuned BERT outputs a label. A summary is a text, of any length.
From Session 1: the encoder-decoder reads a whole input sequence and writes an output sequence one token at a time, with cross-attention to the input. Translation was English → Marathi; summarisation is long English → short English.
Two questions today: how to produce a summary, and how to know whether it is any good.

The second question is as hard as the first. There is no single correct summary of an article; two people write different ones. Evaluation has to live with that.

Extractive vs abstractive

Extractive: the highlighter

Choose the most important sentences of the article and copy them as they are.

Every sentence is grammatical and faithful to the source. But the result can be choppy, repeat itself, or contain "he" without saying who.

Abstractive: in your own words

Write new sentences that say what the article says, as a person would.

Fluent, short, can combine facts from several sentences. But it can hallucinate: state things the article never said.

Exam revision: extractive is underlining the textbook; abstractive is writing your own notes. Underlining cannot introduce mistakes; your notes can, but they are shorter and easier to read.

Extractive methods dominated until about 2017; neural encoder-decoders made abstractive summaries practical, and pretrained models like BART and T5 made them good. Many products still use extractive methods where faithfulness matters most, such as legal and medical text.

How abstractive are the human summaries? In our 500 evaluation articles, of the summary bigrams (word pairs) never appear in the article, and of the summary words are not in the article. The writers rephrase; a pure copy-paste system cannot match them exactly.

The Inshorts summaries are 60-word rewrites by editors. That makes them a good test: extractive methods are limited because the reference uses different words. CNN/DM highlights are more extractive in style, which is why Lead-3 is so strong on them.

Real text needs cleaning first

As downloaded

After four regular expressions

From Day 1, Session 2: curly quotes were lost in the file's encoding and became ?, and sentences were glued together ("media.The"). A sentence splitter would see one giant sentence, and extractive summarisation works on sentences. So: restore apostrophes and quotes, put a space after full stops, then split with sent_tokenize.

The four patterns: ?s and ?t after a letter become an apostrophe; ? at the start of a word becomes an opening quote; ? after a letter or comma before a space becomes a closing quote; a full stop directly followed by a capital letter gets a space. The C1 notebook prints before-and-after for students to check. Do not remove real question marks blindly: some sentences are questions.

Two extractive baselines

Lead-3

Take the first three sentences. That is all.

Why it works: news is written as an inverted pyramid, the most important facts first. Every summariser must beat Lead-3 to be worth anything.

TextRank

  1. Turn each sentence into a TF-IDF vector (Day 1).
  2. Link every pair of sentences with their cosine similarity: a graph.
  3. Run PageRank: a sentence is important if it is similar to other important sentences.
  4. Keep the top 3, in article order.

PageRank on the web: a page is important if important pages link to it. TextRank: a sentence is central if it shares content with many other central sentences, so it carries the main story.

TextRank is from Mihalcea and Tarau (2004). PageRank update: score = (1 - d)/n + d × sum over neighbours of (similarity share × neighbour's score), with damping d = 0.85, repeated until it stops changing. No training data is needed: it works on one article alone.

ROUGE: count the overlap with a human summary

measurecountscaptures
ROUGE-1single words shared with the referencecontent: are the right words there?
ROUGE-2word pairs (bigrams) sharedsome fluency and phrasing
ROUGE-Llongest common subsequence: words in the same order, gaps allowedsentence-level structure

Recall

overlap / words in the reference. ROUGE = Recall-Oriented, the original idea.

Precision

overlap / words in the candidate. Punishes long, padded summaries.

F1

Harmonic mean of the two (Day 2). What libraries usually report.

ROUGE is from Lin (2004). The rouge_score library reports precision, recall and F-measure for each; papers usually quote F-measure, multiplied by 100. With use_stemmer=True, "wins" and "win" count as the same word (both stem to "win"), but "won" does not: stemming is not lemmatization (Day 1, Session 2).

Compute ROUGE (and BLEU) yourself

Start with the given pair. Then rewrite the candidate with synonyms ("triumphed" instead of "won"): ROUGE falls although the meaning is the same. Then make a candidate that copies the reference words but changes a fact ("Australia beat India"): ROUGE stays high although it is false. Those two experiments are the whole critique of ROUGE.

BLEU: ROUGE's cousin from translation

How BLEU works

Precision of 1-, 2-, 3- and 4-grams of the candidate against one or more references (each n-gram counted at most as often as it appears in a reference), combined by a geometric mean, times a brevity penalty for candidates shorter than the reference.

ROUGE vs BLEU

BLEU: precision, built for translation (Papineni et al., 2002): "is what you wrote in the reference?"

ROUGE: recall first, built for summaries: "did you cover what the reference says?"

Both count surface words. Neither knows synonyms, paraphrase, or whether a statement is true. Use them to compare systems on the same data, and always read a sample of outputs yourself.

The syllabus lists BLEU and ROUGE together under evaluation metrics. The calculator on the previous slide also shows BLEU for the same pair. Newer metrics compare meaning with embeddings (BERTScore), but ROUGE remains the standard number reported for summarisation.

Our extractive results, and published ones

On Inshorts, Lead-3 beats TextRank: position matters more than centrality in news. Even the oracle (the best 3 sentences chosen by looking at the reference, a ceiling for any extractive method) is limited, because the editors rephrase.

On CNN/DM, BART beats Lead-3 by about 4 ROUGE-1 points: a large gap in this field. Scores from different datasets cannot be compared with each other.

Inshorts rows: computed for this deck on 500 articles of 150 to 800 words, ROUGE F-measure with stemming, summary of 3 sentences. CNN/DM rows: Lewis et al. (2020), the BART paper. Students compute BART or T5 on Inshorts and CNN/DM in the C1 notebook and add their own rows.

Abstractive models: BART and T5

BART (Facebook, 2020)

An encoder-decoder pretrained as a denoiser: corrupt a text (mask spans, delete words, shuffle sentences), then rebuild the original. Writing the whole text back teaches it to generate.

facebook/bart-large-cnn: BART fine-tuned on CNN/DM, ready to summarise news. Input limit 1,024 tokens.

T5 (Google, 2020)

"Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer": every task is text in, text out, chosen by a prefix:

"summarize: <article>"
"translate English to German: ..."

t5-small (60 M) runs on a CPU; input limit 512 tokens.

From Session 2: BERT was pretrained to fill single masked tokens and cannot write. BART and T5 are pretrained to write out whole spans, which is why they summarise.

T5's pretraining objective is span corruption: replace random spans with sentinel tokens and generate the missing spans. Both models were then fine-tuned on summarisation data. The C1 notebook uses bart-large-cnn (best quality) and t5-small (fast), so students can compare quality and speed.

How the decoder chooses words: greedy vs beam search

Made-up probabilities in a tiny tree, to see the mechanism. Greedy takes the best word at each step and ends with a lower total probability; a beam of 2 keeps two partial summaries alive and finds the better one. bart-large-cnn uses a beam of 4 by default. Larger beams cost more time and tend to give shorter, more generic text.

Summarise with a pretrained model, line by line

from transformers import pipeline
summarizer = pipeline("summarization", model="facebook/bart-large-cnn", device=0)  # device=0: first GPU

result = summarizer(article,
                    max_length=80,           # at most 80 tokens of summary
                    min_length=30,           # at least 30 tokens
                    num_beams=4,            # beam search with 4 candidates
                    no_repeat_ngram_size=3, # never repeat the same 3 words
                    truncation=True)          # cut articles longer than 1,024 tokens
result[0]["summary_text"]

t5 = pipeline("summarization", model="t5-small")          # the pipeline adds "summarize: " itself

Length is a setting

min_length and max_length are in tokens, not words. Inshorts summaries are about 60 words, roughly 75 to 80 tokens.

Long documents

Beyond 1,024 tokens the rest is ignored. For long reports: split into chunks, summarise each, then summarise the summaries.

The chunk-then-combine approach is how the syllabus case study of legal document summarisation is usually handled, since judgments run to tens of thousands of words. It is sometimes called map-reduce summarisation.

Common mistakes in this session

Skipping sentence cleaning. Glued sentences break every extractive method.

Reporting ROUGE without saying which. State ROUGE-1/2/L, F-measure, stemming, dataset.

Comparing ROUGE across datasets. 40 on CNN/DM and 40 on Inshorts mean different things.

Trusting a high ROUGE. A wrong fact in reference words still scores high.

Forgetting the input limit. BART reads 1,024 tokens, T5 512; the rest is silently dropped.

Running BART on a CPU over hundreds of articles. Use a GPU, or t5-small and a small sample.

The first and last are the two that cost students the most time in the lab.

Recap, and what Session 5 starts with

  • Extractive copies sentences (Lead-3, TextRank); abstractive writes new text (BART, T5).
  • ROUGE-1/2/L count word, bigram and in-order overlap with a human summary; BLEU is the precision-first cousin.
  • Decoders generate token by token; beam search keeps several candidates.
  • Judge faithfulness, not only ROUGE.
Still unsolved: each model so far does one job it was fine-tuned for. Could one very large model do summarisation, classification and question answering from an instruction alone, and chat about our own documents? Session 5: large language models, prompting, and retrieval.

Handoff: from task-specific encoder-decoders to general decoder-only LLMs.
